This paper investigates the comparative efficiency of actor-critic methods and direct policy search algorithms in the context of reinforcement learning, with a particular focus on sample complexity—the number of environment interactions required to achieve satisfactory policy performance. The study formulates a consistent experimental framework to evaluate both approaches across a suite of benchmark tasks characterized by varying dimensions and reward structures. We implement representative algorithms from both paradigms using standardized hyperparameter tuning procedures and assess convergence rates, stability, and scalability. Our empirical results reveal that actor-critic methods tend to achieve faster convergence on high-dimensional tasks due to their ability to simultaneously leverage value function estimation and policy gradients. Conversely, direct policy search exhibits greater robustness in scenarios where value function approximation is challenging or introduces significant bias. The analysis highlights the trade-offs in sample efficiency, generalization capability, and computational overhead inherent to each method. This work provides actionable insights for practitioners on method selection tailored to task properties, and contributes to the broader understanding of reinforcement learning algorithm design. Keywords: reinforcement learning, actor-critic, direct policy search, sample complexity, policy optimization, value function, convergence analysis.
