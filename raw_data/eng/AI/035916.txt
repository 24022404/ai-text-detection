This paper investigates the composition properties of inferential privacy in the context of time-series data, a rapidly growing area in information technology due to the prevalence of sensor networks, smart devices, and online activity monitoring. The objective of the study is to understand how privacy guarantees accumulate or degrade when multiple inferential privacy-preserving mechanisms are applied to time-dependent datasets. We develop a formal framework to model the application of inferential privacy techniques over sequential and overlapping time windows, analyzing how inter-temporal correlations affect overall privacy risks. Our methods employ probabilistic modeling, privacy budget allocation strategies, and information-theoretic measures to quantify privacy loss under various composition scenarios, including both sequential and parallel combinations. Experimental evaluation on synthetic and real-world time-series datasets demonstrates that naively applying traditional composition rules may underestimate privacy leakage, especially in the presence of high temporal correlations. Our findings reveal new upper and lower bounds for privacy loss, enabling practitioners to design more robust privacy-preserving systems for continuous data streams. The research contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of inferential privacy dynamics and provides practical guidance for deploying privacy-preserving analytics in domains such as health monitoring, smart grids, and location-based services. Keywords: inferential privacy, time-series data, privacy composition, temporal correlations, privacy-preserving analytics, information security.
