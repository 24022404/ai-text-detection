Continuous-Time Bayesian Networks (CTBNs) are powerful tools for modeling complex temporal systems where state transitions occur at arbitrary, continuous points in time. However, existing CTBN frameworks often lack mechanisms to explicitly represent clock-driven or periodic behaviors frequently encountered in real-world systems such as industrial automation, healthcare monitoring, and cyber-physical infrastructures. This paper introduces Continuous-Time Bayesian Networks with Clocks (CTBN-C), an extended modeling formalism that integrates explicit clock variables into the CTBN structure. Our approach augments traditional CTBNs by incorporating clocks to model periodic or scheduled events alongside stochastic transitions, enabling richer and more accurate representations of hybrid temporal dynamics.

We propose a novel formalism for clock variable representation within the CTBN framework, along with a scalable inference algorithm that adapts standard sampling and variational methods to efficiently handle both clock-driven and regular transition mechanisms. Through comprehensive experiments on synthetic benchmark datasets and real-world case studies, our results demonstrate that CTBN-C significantly improves temporal prediction accuracy and event detection performance compared to conventional CTBNs and other continuous-time probabilistic models. The inclusion of clocks offers enhanced expressiveness for hybrid-event modeling, reduces temporal abstraction errors, and enables effective reasoning over both regular and irregular system trajectories.

Our work advances the state-of-the-art in probabilistic temporal modeling by bridging the gap between stochastic and scheduled processes, with implications for domains such as reliability analysis, predictive maintenance, and intelligent monitoring. Key contributions include the CTBN-C model, efficient inference techniques, and empirical validation across diverse application scenarios. Keywords: continuous-time Bayesian networks, temporal modeling, clock variables, probabilistic inference, hybrid systems, event prediction.
