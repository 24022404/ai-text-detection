Objective: Automotive radar classification systems must balance hardware cost, complexity, and performance. This study investigates the impact of sensor resolution on object classification accuracy by comparing a widely available off-the-shelf radar against a custom experimental high-resolution radar. Our goal is to determine the minimum resolution required to achieve reliable classification in real-world driving scenarios.

Methods: We collected synchronized radar data from urban and highway environments using both sensors. Range-Doppler and range-azimuth maps were generated and processed by a convolutional neural network (CNN) classifier optimized for radar signatures. We systematically varied angular and range resolutions in the experimental radar data to simulate intermediate configurations. Classification tasks included distinguishing pedestrians, cyclists, cars, and larger vehicles under diverse clutter and motion conditions.

Results: The off-the-shelf sensor achieved 82.3% overall accuracy, while the full-resolution experimental radar reached 90.7%. Incremental improvements plateaued beyond an angular resolution of 1.5°, with diminishing returns on accuracy (<1% gain) for finer settings. Range resolution showed a similar trend, with most gains realized up to 0.5 m. Under low signal-to-noise conditions, higher resolution improved robustness by up to 5%.

Conclusion: Our findings demonstrate that moderate increases in radar resolution yield significant classification benefits, but excessive resolution adds hardware cost without proportional performance gains. We provide practical guidelines for radar system designers, showing that an angular resolution of 1.5° and range resolution of 0.5 m suffice for high-accuracy automotive classification, enabling cost-effective sensor choices in advanced driver assistance systems.

Keywords: automotive radar, sensor resolution, object classification, range-Doppler, convolutional neural network, ADAS.
