Abstract:

Objective: Despite rapid advancements in image processing, the evaluation of algorithms remains heavily reliant on standard quantitative metrics such as PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio), SSIM (Structural Similarity Index), and MSE (Mean Squared Error). However, these metrics often fail to capture perceptual quality and context-dependent nuances, leading to discrepancies between numerical scores and human visual assessment. This paper aims to systematically investigate and illustrate the common limitations and pitfalls of prevailing image processing metrics.

Methods: We conduct a series of controlled image manipulation experiments representing a range of tasks, including denoising, super-resolution, and compression. Through comparative case studies and visual examples, we demonstrate situations where widely used metrics provide misleading results. A qualitative analysis is complemented by insights into metric sensitivity, bias, and lack of perceptual alignment.

Results: Our findings reveal that many standard metrics are inadequate in several common scenarios, such as high-frequency noise retention, structural distortions, and semantic changes, which may be penalized or rewarded counterintuitively. We present pictorial evidence highlighting situations where metric scores diverge from human subjective perception, and quantify inconsistencies via user studies.

Conclusion: This study underscores the necessity of incorporating both objective and subjective assessment strategies when evaluating image processing algorithms. By exposing the shortcomings of conventional metrics through intuitive 'picture stories,' we advocate for the development and adoption of more robust, perceptually-aware evaluation techniques. Our work serves as a resource for researchers and practitioners, prompting critical reflection on the choice and interpretation of image quality metrics.

Keywords: image quality metrics, PSNR, SSIM, MSE, perceptual quality, computer vision, image processing, evaluation limitations, visual assessment, algorithm benchmarking.
