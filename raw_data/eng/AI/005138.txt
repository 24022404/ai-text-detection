The Information Bottleneck (IB) principle provides a theoretical framework for learning efficient representations by balancing prediction accuracy and representation complexity. This paper investigates the learnability aspects of the IB, focusing on the generalization performance achievable under different training conditions and model architectures. We analyze the sample complexity of learning IB representations, deriving bounds on the number of examples needed to achieve a desired level of generalization. Our approach leverages tools from statistical learning theory and rate-distortion theory to quantify the trade-off between compression and prediction. We present novel theoretical results demonstrating how the architectural properties of the encoder and decoder networks influence the learnability of the IB objective. Empirical evaluations on synthetic and real-world datasets validate our theoretical findings, showing that our derived bounds accurately predict the generalization performance of IB-trained models. Furthermore, we explore the implications of our results for designing more efficient and robust representation learning algorithms. Key words: Information Bottleneck, Learnability, Generalization, Representation Learning, Sample Complexity, Rate-Distortion Theory.
