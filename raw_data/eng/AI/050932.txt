Objective: Modern deep neural networks often exhibit miscalibration, producing overconfident or underconfident probability estimates that can undermine reliability in safety‐critical and decision-support applications. This paper revisits the calibration properties of state-of-the-art architectures across vision and language tasks, aiming to (1) quantify miscalibration trends as models scale in depth and data size, and (2) evaluate the effectiveness of existing and novel calibration techniques.

Methods: We conduct a systematic empirical study on convolutional (ResNet, DenseNet) and transformer-based (Vision Transformer, BERT) models using benchmark datasets (CIFAR-100, ImageNet, GLUE). Calibration is measured via Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and reliability diagrams. We compare standard post-hoc methods (temperature scaling, isotonic regression) against two new approaches: adaptive temperature scaling that conditions on feature-space activations, and mixup-based calibration regularization during training.

Results: Our experiments reveal that calibration often degrades as model capacity increases, despite improved accuracy. Standard temperature scaling reduces ECE by up to 50%, but struggles on highly overparameterized transformers. The proposed adaptive scaling further cuts ECE by 20%–30% relative to vanilla temperature methods. Mixup regularization yields consistent calibration gains (up to 35% ECE reduction) without sacrificing accuracy. Combining both techniques achieves the lowest miscalibration across all tested models.

Conclusion: Revisiting calibration in modern networks uncovers systematic overconfidence trends and demonstrates that tailored post-hoc and training-time interventions can substantially improve predictive reliability. These findings inform best practices for deploying deep models in applications demanding trustworthy uncertainty estimates.

Keywords: neural network calibration, model uncertainty, temperature scaling, mixup regularization, deep learning reliability.
