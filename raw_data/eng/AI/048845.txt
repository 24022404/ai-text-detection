Graph kernels are foundational methods for measuring similarity between graphs, enabling effective graph-based machine learning in domains such as bioinformatics, social network analysis, and chemical informatics. Two primary approaches underlie graph kernel computations: explicit feature maps, which represent graphs through structured feature vectors, and implicit feature maps, which leverage kernel functions to operate in high-dimensional feature spaces without direct enumeration. Despite their widespread use, the relationship between these paradigms remains insufficiently understood, limiting both theoretical analysis and practical advancements. 

This paper presents a unifying framework that systematically elucidates the connections between explicit and implicit feature maps for a broad class of graph kernels. We introduce formal definitions and mathematical models that bridge these views, highlighting commonalities and identifying conditions under which an explicit representation can be efficiently derived from its implicit form, and vice versa. Additionally, we analyze computational complexities and memory requirements, offering new insights into kernel scalability for large and complex graphs.

Our empirical evaluation covers multiple state-of-the-art graph kernels, demonstrating how our framework informs kernel selection and enables optimization across diverse datasets. Experimental results show that applying our unifying perspective can lead to significant improvements in model interpretability and computational efficiency, without sacrificing predictive accuracy.

By providing a comprehensive theoretical and empirical analysis, our work advances the understanding of graph kernel designs and facilitates new developments in graph-based machine learning. Keywords: graph kernels, explicit feature maps, implicit feature maps, kernel methods, graph classification, machine learning, computational efficiency.
