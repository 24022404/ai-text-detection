This research addresses the need for more transparent and interpretable models in medical imaging by developing a framework for explainable disease classification that utilizes weakly-supervised segmentation techniques. The primary aim is to provide accurate disease diagnosis while offering insights into the decision-making process of the model.

Methods/Approach: We introduce an innovative approach that leverages weakly-supervised segmentation to enhance the explainability of disease classification models. Our framework utilizes a combination of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and attention mechanisms to highlight critical regions in medical images that influence classification decisions. The model is trained using only image-level labels, reducing the need for extensive pixel-wise annotations traditionally required for segmentation tasks.

Results/Findings: The proposed method demonstrates superior performance in disease classification compared to traditional CNN-based approaches while providing clear visual explanations for its predictions. Our experiments on multiple benchmark datasets indicate that the model achieves high accuracy and robustness, with visualizations that effectively highlight medically relevant areas associated with disease presence. The weakly-supervised segmentation enhances the interpretability of the classification process substantially.

Conclusion/Implications: This study provides a significant contribution to the domain of explainable artificial intelligence in medical imaging by presenting a method that balances prediction accuracy with interpretability. The integration of weakly-supervised segmentation expands the potential for clinicians to trust and adopt AI-driven diagnostic tools, potentially improving clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. Our framework sets a precedent for developing more explainable models in various medical applications.

Key Keywords: explainable AI, disease classification, weakly-supervised segmentation, convolutional neural networks, medical imaging, interpretable models, attention mechanisms.
